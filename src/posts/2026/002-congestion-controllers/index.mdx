*This is part one of a two-part series. [Part two builds the adaptive controller.](/posts/adaptive-congestion-control)*

When one service calls another, sending requests faster is not always better. As downstream workers approach capacity, queues form, latency rises, and requests begin to miss their deadlines. Retrying those requests creates more demand at exactly the moment the service has the least capacity to spare. Push hard enough and useful throughput can fall even as every part of the system gets busier.

Backpressure can protect the service, but it leaves the caller with a difficult decision. It must estimate how much work each downstream worker can accept, decide where the next request should go, and sometimes wait instead of sending immediately. That decision becomes harder when both services are horizontally scaled, processing time varies, and no replica has a complete view of the system.

In this first part, we will build that decision from first principles. We will make the queues visible, distribute requests across independently queued workers, and compare fixed rate with fixed concurrency as the client and worker fleets change.

By the end, we will have a realistic request path and a way to recognise the useful operating point of each worker. [Part two](/posts/adaptive-congestion-control) turns those measurements into adaptive per-worker limits and paced admissions, then deals with fairness, stale observations, and production scaling.

## Prelude: why not just send everything?

Imagine two services in the same distributed system. The first receives or produces work and calls the second to complete part of it. We will call them the **client service** and the **worker service**, but those names describe their roles in this one interaction. The client service may itself be a worker for something further upstream.

Both sides are horizontally scaled. Several independent client instances send requests to several independent workers. Each worker has finite processing capacity and a small queue. The network adds some latency, endpoint discovery changes over time, and individual workers sometimes become faster or slower. No client knows exactly how much total demand exists, and no worker knows how much work all of the clients are about to send.

The tempting policy is to send every request as soon as it arrives. If the selected worker cannot accept it, return an error and let the caller retry.

This works while demand is comfortably below capacity. More requests produce more completed work, so the system appears to reward us for driving it harder. The problem begins near saturation. A worker can only process so many requests at once; additional requests must wait. Every waiting request consumes time and usually some combination of memory, connection state, task state, and deadline budget without increasing the worker’s useful throughput.

Removing an explicit application queue does not remove this waiting. It moves it. Requests can accumulate in a caller, a connection pool, an HTTP/2 stream limit, a socket buffer, a load balancer, an executor, or the operating-system scheduler. The caller experiences all of these locations as increased latency, even when the worker’s own queue appears empty.

Eventually requests wait longer than their deadlines. Some may still reach the worker and consume resources even though the caller no longer wants their answers. Others are cancelled, rejected, or retried. A retry is particularly dangerous during overload: it turns evidence that the system has too much work into another piece of work. If many callers make the same reasonable local decision, they can keep a recovering service overloaded long after the original burst has ended.

Real services can also lose useful capacity under pressure. They spend more time managing queues, allocating memory, switching tasks, contending on locks, cleaning up cancelled work, and producing responses that arrive too late to be useful. Offered load continues to rise while completed, useful throughput flattens or even falls.

The alternative is to apply backpressure before every request enters the worker service. When a worker appears busy, the client can hold a request briefly and admit it when capacity becomes available. Waiting at the client makes the queue intentional: it can be bounded, included in the request deadline, cancelled before consuming downstream resources, and paced so that released work does not arrive in another burst.

This does not mean waiting forever. If the client-side queue is full or the request cannot be dispatched within its deadline, rejection is the honest outcome. Otherwise backpressure would merely exchange server overload for unbounded memory growth and enormous latency in the caller. Retries must enter the same admission path as original attempts; they cannot be allowed to bypass the very mechanism protecting the service.

So our problem is not whether requests should ever wait. They inevitably will. The questions are:

- Where should they wait?
- How much waiting work should be allowed?
- Which worker should receive the next request?
- What observation tells a client to slow down—or that it is safe to speed up again?

The rest of the article will answer those questions by changing one part of a running system at a time. The clock will continue to move, but every experiment will ask you to perturb the system and watch how it responds.

## 1. A limit that worked yesterday

Start with one client and four workers. Every coloured dot is a request, and every worker has a small queue of its own. The client samples two workers and uses its own latency and in-flight observations to choose between them. That routing policy stays the same throughout these experiments.

First we limit how often requests can start. Each client may send at most half a request per second to each worker. This is a local budget: adding another client adds another set of budgets. No existing client is told to give up part of its allowance.

Each interactive section introduces a fresh copy of the same simulator, already configured for the idea beside it. The controls do not perform a scripted demonstration: the experiment describes an outcome, and you must decide which ordinary client, worker, processing-time, or controller control will produce it. The checks turn green only after the simulator observes the result.

Watch **completed throughput** as well as offered load. More arrivals do not necessarily mean more useful work. The queue shows waiting directly, while duration includes the time successful requests spend getting to the worker, waiting, and being processed.

The rate and concurrency experiments later in the article begin from the same healthy one-client topology and apply the same processing-time increase. A rate budget does not shrink when work becomes slower. A concurrency permit stays occupied until the request finishes, so the rate of new admissions falls naturally. Compare their responses rather than treating the absolute numbers as a controller benchmark.

Concurrency connects rate and duration: in steady state, average work in flight equals completed throughput multiplied by average latency. A fixed concurrency limit therefore reduces the sending rate as latency rises. An adaptive controller goes further by changing the target itself; pacing turns that potentially fractional target into spaced-out requests.

We now have the central question for the rest of the article: **which observation should make a client change its limit?** Loss provides an explicit signal, but only after a queue refuses work. Delay can warn earlier, but separating service time from queueing—and sharing capacity fairly—takes more care.

The rest of the article follows that question through increasingly realistic models. Each step keeps the routing topology fixed unless it is the thing being studied, so an improvement in admission control is not accidentally credited to a better load balancer.

## Act one: learn to see congestion

### 2. Make the work visible

A request is not an abstract counter. From the client’s point of view it begins when the call is admitted and ends when the response returns. In between, it passes through several distinct stages:

1. travel from the client to the chosen worker;
2. wait in that worker’s queue;
3. receive service from the worker; and
4. travel back to the client.

Only the third stage performs the useful work that the worker exists to do. All four consume the request’s deadline, and the client normally observes only their sum.

That distinction matters whenever we interpret latency. Suppose a worker takes one second to process a request. An observed duration of three seconds does not mean the operation itself became three times as expensive. The request may have spent two seconds waiting behind earlier work. Conversely, a worker can become slower without forming a queue if admissions fall quickly enough. The same end-to-end metric can therefore describe different internal states.

Throughout the visualisations, moving dots are requests. A dot crossing the middle is in the network or being routed; a dot parked in front of a worker is queued; a dot attached to a worker is being processed. Pausing freezes the motion without changing the model. This is not just decoration: the physical position of a request explains why its measured duration can be much greater than its service time.

Once we can see the stages, queueing latency stops being mysterious. It is simply stored unfinished work, paid for one request at a time.

### 3. Name the health signals

We need more than one number to describe whether this system is behaving well. Two familiar sets of signals give us a useful vocabulary.

**RED** describes the request path:

- **Rate:** how much work is offered and how much completes.
- **Errors:** how much work is rejected or fails.
- **Duration:** how long successful work takes, usually including tail percentiles such as p99.

**USE** describes the worker resources:

- **Utilisation:** how much of the available service capacity is busy.
- **Saturation:** how much work is waiting for that capacity.
- **Errors:** whether the resource itself is failing work.

Neither family is sufficient alone. A worker can be fully utilised while every request completes with excellent latency; 100% utilisation is not automatically an incident. A request path can also be slow while workers appear under-utilised because the waiting occurs in a client, connection pool, or network queue that the worker cannot see.

The simulator therefore avoids one synthetic “health” score. The top-level metrics report completed and offered rate, rejection, duration, utilisation, and queue depth separately. Endpoint details then break those observations down by client and worker. When a later controller improves p99 latency, we will still ask whether it did so by preserving useful throughput or merely by leaving workers idle.

This vocabulary also makes recovery less ambiguous. Errors may stop as soon as a bounded queue is no longer full, while latency remains elevated until the existing queue drains. A single green light would hide that interval. RED and USE let us say precisely what recovered, and what has not.

## Act two: build a realistic request path

### 4. Open the load balancer

It is tempting to draw a horizontally scaled service as one queue feeding a bank of interchangeable workers. That model quietly gives the load balancer powers that real deployments often do not have.

Workers normally accept connections or requests independently. Each has its own executor, connection limits, and bounded queue. Once a request has been sent to worker 1, worker 2 cannot reach across the network and take it simply because it became idle first. A proxy can maintain a centralised queue, but that is a deliberate architecture with its own memory, availability, fairness, and head-of-line-blocking trade-offs—not a free property of load balancing.

Our clients instead learn a set of worker endpoints and choose one for each request. A network load balancer may still provide reachability, but the client owns the scheduling decision and every queued request is attached to the worker already selected for it.

This creates a state that aggregate dashboards can obscure: one worker can be idle while another still has queued work. Total capacity exists, but it is in the wrong place for the decisions we already made. Admission control and placement must therefore be designed together.

### 5. Random placement can waste capacity

Uniform random placement is attractive because it is simple, stateless, and unbiased over a long period. Unfortunately, requests do not experience the long-run average. They experience one particular sequence of choices.

Even when offered load is below total service capacity, independent random choices sometimes cluster on the same worker. With short queues and variable service times, those small clusters create head-of-line waiting while another worker is ready. The average throughput may still look fine, but the unlucky requests inflate the latency tail.

Making the queue global would hide this placement error by allowing the next available worker to take any job. In our per-worker topology the client needs to avoid creating the imbalance in the first place. It needs a better choice that remains cheap and decentralized.

### 6. Two random choices are surprisingly powerful

The “Power of Two Choices” rule makes one small change: sample two distinct workers at random, compare them, and send the request to the better candidate. That extra observation dramatically reduces accidental concentration while retaining constant decision cost and avoiding a scan across the whole fleet.

We still need to define “better.” Choosing the endpoint with the lowest raw latency is not enough. A naturally fast worker may already have many requests in flight, while a slightly slower worker is idle. A useful first approximation is:

```text
score = (in_flight + 1) × smoothed_RTT
```

The `+1` estimates the cost of the request we are about to add. Multiplying by recent round-trip time combines how much work is ahead of us with roughly how expensive that work has been. The score is deliberately local: two clients can assign different scores to the same worker because they have seen different requests.

Power of Two will be the common routing layer for the rest of the article. When we compare rate, concurrency, and adaptive controllers, they all receive the same two candidates and the same placement rule. That keeps us from crediting admission control for an improvement that actually came from better routing.

*Reference:* [Mitzenmacher’s original treatment of the Power of Two Choices](https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/1996/7979.html) develops the randomized-allocation result behind this routing rule.

### 7. Every endpoint needs local memory

A service-wide average cannot support that decision. The client needs an endpoint table containing at least:

- requests currently in flight;
- a recent latency estimate;
- recent successes and failures;
- the age of the last observation; and
- eventually, that endpoint’s congestion-control state.

This table exists per client. With `C` clients and `W` workers, there are `C × W` independently evolving client–worker relationships. Two clients may hold different RTT estimates and different limits for the same worker because neither observes the other’s requests directly.

Newly discovered endpoints begin cold. They must not silently inherit the fleet average: a replacement instance might have different hardware, a cold cache, or a failure mode that the existing workers do not share. Nor should a cold endpoint be treated as infinitely bad and starved of the requests needed to measure it.

Routing and congestion control use this same memory but answer different questions. Routing asks, “which of these candidates looks better?” Congestion control asks, “is it safe to send another request there at all?” Keeping those questions separate will become important when both sampled workers are busy.

## Act three: fixed limits meet a changing fleet

### 8. The fixed-rate baseline

A rate limiter answers a narrow question: how often may this client start work? It does not care whether the selected worker is fast, slow, idle, queued, or recently replaced. That simplicity is useful. A fixed rate is easy to reason about, easy to enforce, and often entirely adequate when the environment is stable.

We apply the limit per worker rather than dividing one global client budget across the discovered endpoints. If one client may send `0.5` requests per second to each of four workers, its maximum aggregate rate is `2/s`. Adding a worker adds another `0.5/s` budget, allowing the client to discover new service capacity without reconfiguring its global rate.

The cost of that convenience is an assumption: `0.5/s` must be safe for every worker and the total contributed by every client must remain safe for the fleet. The number was tuned against one particular client count, worker count, and processing-time distribution. Nothing in the limiter tells it when those assumptions stop being true.

### 9. Horizontal clients multiply the rate

A reasonable local rate becomes a much larger system-wide rate when the client service scales horizontally. Every new client creates its own endpoint table and its own complete set of rate budgets. Existing clients do not know that they should surrender part of theirs.

With four workers and a `0.5/s` per-endpoint rate, one client can offer `2/s`. Two clients can offer `4/s`; eight can offer `16/s`. Autoscaling the caller has multiplied pressure on the callee even though no configuration value changed.

The checks deliberately require a sustained observation. A queue containing one request for a single tick may be harmless scheduling noise; a queue that stays occupied for five seconds is a changed operating point. Notice that completed throughput eventually stops tracking offered throughput. Extra admissions are buying delay and rejection, not more useful capacity.

A fixed per-client rate therefore encodes an assumption about client count that autoscaling is free to invalidate.

### 10. More workers change the answer too

Scaling the worker service changes the correct answer in the other direction. A single global rate cannot use new capacity unless someone increases it. A per-worker rate discovers added capacity naturally, but removing workers also removes their budgets and concentrates existing work onto the survivors.

Removal has awkward transient semantics too. Requests already assigned to a departing worker cannot be teleported into another queue. They must finish while the endpoint drains, be cancelled, or fail and re-enter the normal retry and admission path. Pretending otherwise makes scale-down look much safer than it is.

We now have incompatible requirements for a static number. It should grow when workers are added, shrink when clients are added, and change again when either side is removed. Replica counts are only proxies for the capacity and demand we actually care about. Even perfect discovery cannot make a fixed rate adapt to the next problem: a worker whose speed changes in place.

### 11. Processing time is not constant

Capacity changes even when replica counts do not. Cache misses, noisy neighbours, garbage collection, heterogeneous hardware, and differences in request cost all change service time. A rate that was conservative a moment ago can become excessive without any deployment or discovery event.

The next experiment begins below capacity. Keep the controller and topology fixed, start the simulation, and change service time from one second to three seconds. The configured arrival rate will not move. Watch utilisation, waiting, duration, and finally rejection respond in that order.

The first visible effect is not necessarily an error. Workers remain busy for longer, so the same arrivals begin to occupy their queues. Duration rises before the hard queue limit is reached; only then does rejection appear. A loss-only alarm sees the last stage of the problem.

Rate controls arrivals, but it does not directly bound the amount of unfinished work created by slower responses.

### 12. The bounded queues have a job to do

A bounded queue converts unlimited waiting into explicit rejection. That protects memory, connections, and deadline budget from growing without limit. It also provides an unambiguous signal that a particular worker could not accept more work.

The bound is protection, not a target. A queue does not need to be full before it becomes harmful. Every occupied slot represents a request paying additional latency without increasing the worker’s instantaneous throughput. When demand falls, rejection stops as soon as space reappears, but duration remains elevated until the old work drains.

This is why the simulator attributes rejections to the selected worker and keeps them separate from cancellations caused by topology changes. A controller can learn from a worker refusing work; it should not interpret a client being removed as evidence that the worker is overloaded.

Loss is therefore both valuable and late. We should use it, but we can avoid depending on it as our first congestion signal.

### 13. Replace rate with work in flight

A concurrency limit bounds requests that have started but not finished. A permit is associated with one endpoint and is returned only when that request completes or is rejected. A slow endpoint cannot consume the permits of a healthy neighbour.

This small change makes the admission rate sensitive to duration. If requests take three times as long, each permit remains occupied for roughly three times as long, so the client naturally starts work less often. We have not measured capacity or changed the configured limit; feedback arrives through the lifetime of the permit itself.

The fixed window does not make the worker faster. Instead, offered rate falls because unfinished requests retain their places in the window. In this scenario that keeps the server queues empty and avoids sustained rejection.

Concurrency is already a feedback mechanism, but it is incomplete. Someone still has to choose the size of the window. Too small wastes capacity; too large recreates the queue we were trying to avoid.

### 14. Little’s Law connects the measurements

Little’s Law relates the quantities we can observe:

```text
L = λW
```

`L` is the average number of requests in the system, `λ` is completed throughput, and `W` is average time in the system. If an endpoint completes one request per second with an average round-trip time of 1.3 seconds, it will have about 1.3 requests in flight on average.

This explains why a fixed concurrency window becomes an implicit variable rate: rearranging the equation gives `λ = L / W`. As duration grows while `L` stays fixed, the achievable admission rate falls.

The word “average” is important. A controller target of `1.3` requests is meaningful even though an individual request is indivisible. Rounding it to an integer throws away information and creates abrupt changes around every boundary. Later we will use the target and measured RTT to pace discrete requests at the corresponding average rate.

Little’s Law translates between units. It does not tell us whether `1.3` is a safe operating point. To choose a target, we first need to understand what happens as unfinished work increases.

*Reference:* John Little’s paper, [“A Proof for the Queuing Formula: L = λW”](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/opre.9.3.383), establishes the relationship under broad steady-state conditions.

### 15. Find the knee in each worker

At low concurrency, adding another request in flight can increase throughput by keeping the worker busy across network gaps and scheduling delays. Eventually the worker reaches capacity. Beyond that point, extra concurrency mostly becomes a queue: throughput flattens while duration rises sharply.

The bend between those regions is the **knee**. It is not one universal queue depth or utilisation percentage. A worker’s useful window depends on service time, internal parallelism, network delay, and the shape of its workload. Two nominally identical replicas can have different knees for long periods.

We would like to operate near the knee: enough work in flight to keep capacity useful, but not enough to maintain a costly queue. A manually configured window can hit that point only for the conditions in which it was tuned.

A congestion controller automates the search. It probes upward while the path appears healthy and retreats when its feedback says that added work has become waiting. Different controllers mainly disagree about which signal to trust and how quickly to respond.

## What we have built

We began with hidden waiting and ended with a concrete client-side model: discover workers, sample two candidates, score them from local endpoint state, and admit work through a per-worker concurrency window. Bounded worker queues remain a last line of defence rather than the desired operating point.

Fixed concurrency is a substantial improvement over fixed rate because duration feeds back into admissions automatically. It still leaves one number for a human to choose. Finding the knee showed what an adaptive controller must do next: probe for useful capacity, recognise when added work has become waiting, and retreat without waiting for the queue to fill.
